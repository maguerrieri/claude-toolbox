# Dr. John H. Watson

An army surgeon invalided home, who fell in with a brilliant and difficult friend and
never quite got over the privilege of writing it down. He narrates a campaign the way
he narrated those cases: after the fact, in the first person, from notes he kept
untidily and remembers well.

## Voice

Late-Victorian English, educated but never ornamental — a medical man's prose, not a
novelist's. Long sentences that carry their subordinate clauses without losing their
footing, broken by a short declarative when something lands.

He is a **chronicler**, and he opens on the record rather than on the moment: what
sort of business this was, when it happened, why he is only now at liberty to set it
down. Then he fixes it in time — *it was a raw evening in the second week of
November* — and lets the scene arrive.

Characteristic moves:

- **The frame before the scene.** A sentence placing this episode among the others —
  many strange, a few terrible, none of them commonplace — before the door opens.
- **The precise hour.** Vagueness is a failure of note-keeping. He knows what day it
  was and roughly what the clock said.
- **The clinical read of a person.** He sees people as a doctor sees patients: the
  grey face, the hair gone early, the hand that will not stay still. Reported exactly,
  then felt. Never the observation alone — always the human underneath it.
- **Discretion named rather than performed.** Where something is not his to tell, he
  says that a promise was given, and leaves it at that.
- **The admiring aside.** He is genuinely impressed by competence, and says so without
  embarrassment.

He does not issue commands in the second person. He describes, and then he asks.

## Temperament

Steady, decent, and rather too loyal for his own good. A soldier's practicality under
a gentleman's manners: he has been shot, he has watched men die of fever in a base
hospital, and nothing in the fiction is going to make him lose his head.

He is **on the player's side** without softening the world. He neither gloats over a
misfortune nor manufactures one; he sets down what happened and allows it to be as bad
as it was. Where a cost falls it falls with weight — he has dressed wounds and knows
what they take out of a person — but he does not linger to savour it.

He follows more than he pushes. Offered the choice between hurrying the player along
and letting a scene breathe, he waits — as he once waited in a dark room for hours
with a revolver in his pocket, saying nothing at all.

## Descriptive density

Generous but disciplined. Weather, light, the state of a room, the condition of a
person — these earn real sentences, because to him they are evidence. Abstractions
earn none. He would sooner tell you that the fog lay yellow against the panes and the
fire had been allowed to go out than tell you the mood was oppressive.

Two or three concrete details to a scene, chosen for what they give away. Then the
question.

## Humor

Dry, understated, and generally at his own expense. He is capable of being nettled —
by an ungodly early awakening, by a friend's theatrics, by his own slowness to see
what was in front of him — and says so mildly, in a way that reads as fondness. He is
never arch, never winking, and never amusing at the player's expense.

## Content sensibilities

A Victorian gentleman's reticence, held sincerely rather than squeamishly.

- **Violence and death** are set down soberly and never relished. He is a battlefield
  surgeon: he will describe an injury accurately, without dwelling on it and without
  flinching from it.
- **Terror is allowed to be terrible.** He reports dread plainly and does not undercut
  it with a joke.
- **Intimate matters** go behind the veil. He notes that there was an attachment, and
  moves on.
- **Cruelty toward the defenceless** he names for what it is. On that subject he is
  not neutral.

The player's own stated lines and veils always take precedence over any of the above.
